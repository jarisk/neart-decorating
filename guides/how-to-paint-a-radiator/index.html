How to paint a radiator and which paint to use

Updated 27 November 2026By Neart Decorating, Cardiff8 min read
Quick answer

Turn the heating off and let the radiator go completely cold. Clean it with sugar soap, sand it lightly to give the paint a key, treat any rust and prime bare metal with a metal primer. Then apply two thin coats of radiator enamel or a paint the manufacturer says is suitable for radiators, using a small brush and a mini roller or a radiator aerosol. Ordinary emulsion is not suitable. Leave it at least 24 hours, or as long as the tin says, before turning the heating back on.

What is the best paint for radiators?

A radiator gets hot and cools down every day, so the paint has to flex with the metal and stay white (or whatever colour you choose) without cracking, flaking or yellowing. That rules out standard emulsion. It is made for walls, and on a hot radiator it soon cracks, discolours and wears through.

PaintGood forThings to know
Radiator enamel (tin)Most radiators, especially the front facesMade for heat; resists yellowing better than ordinary gloss. Available in gloss and satin.
Radiator aerosolColumn and designer radiators, grilles, tight gapsQuick and smooth, but needs lots of masking and several light passes to avoid runs.
Water-based satinwood or eggshell for metalMatching the radiator to your woodworkOnly use one the manufacturer says is suitable for radiators. Low smell and quick drying.
Standard emulsionNot suitableCracks and discolours with the heat.

Always check the tin. Heat tolerance, recoat times and suitability for radiators vary between products.

Satin or gloss? A satin finish hides dents, ripples and old brush marks far better than high gloss, which shows every flaw on a pressed-steel panel. Satin also tends to suit modern interiors. Traditional solvent-based whites can yellow with age and heat, which is one reason radiator-specific paints and good water-based paints are now the usual choice.

What tools do you need to paint a radiator?

  • Sugar soap and a sponge, plus a vacuum or a long, thin brush to clear dust from the fins and gaps
  • Abrasive paper: a medium grade for flaky areas and a fine grade for keying the whole surface, or a sanding sponge
  • A wire brush and rust treatment if there is any rust
  • Metal primer for bare or rusty patches
  • A good small brush (around 25–50 mm) and a long-handled radiator brush or roller for the back and the gaps between panels
  • A mini roller with a short-pile or foam sleeve for flat panels
  • Masking tape and dust sheets to protect valves, pipes, the wall and the floor
  • Card or a sheet of paper to slide behind the radiator and protect the wall

How to prepare a radiator for painting

Preparation is what makes radiator paint last. Most peeling and flaking comes from painting over dust, grease or loose old paint.

  1. Turn it off and let it cool. Switch off the heating, or close that radiator’s valve, and wait until the metal is cold. Paint put on a warm radiator dries too fast, drips and leaves brush marks. Check that a timer or smart thermostat won’t switch the heating on while you work.
  2. Protect the area. Lay dust sheets, mask the valves and pipe nuts, and tuck card behind the radiator.
  3. Clean it. Vacuum out the fins and top grille, then wash the whole radiator with sugar soap. Rinse with clean water and let it dry.
  4. Remove loose paint and rust. Scrape off anything flaking. Wire brush rust spots back to sound metal and apply a rust treatment if needed. Don’t paint over rust: it will keep spreading under the new finish.
  5. Key the surface. Lightly sand all the old paint so the new coat can grip, then wipe off the dust with a damp cloth.
  6. Prime bare metal. Spot-prime any bare or treated patches with a metal primer. If the whole radiator is bare, prime all of it.

Old cast-iron radiators: many Victorian and Edwardian terraces in Roath, Canton and Cathays still have their original column radiators, often with decades of paint layers. Paint applied before the 1960s may contain lead. Don’t dry sand, burn off or wire brush old layers without testing them first; a lead test kit from a DIY shop is a sensible start. Cast iron with heavy build-up is often better shot-blasted and powder coated by a specialist.

How to paint a radiator, step by step

  1. Start with the awkward bits. Use the small brush or radiator brush on the edges, the gaps between panels and the top grille first.
  2. Paint the face. Use a mini roller or brush, working from the top down. On a panel radiator, finish with light vertical strokes that follow the channels.
  3. Keep coats thin. Two thin coats look better and last longer than one thick one. Heavy paint runs, sags in the grooves and takes longer to harden.
  4. Check for runs. Look along the bottom edge and the channels a few minutes after each section and brush out any drips while the paint is still wet.
  5. Let it dry, then add the second coat. Go by the recoat time on the tin, which can range from a few hours for quick-drying water-based paints to overnight for solvent-based ones. Lightly de-nib between coats if any dust has settled.
  6. Remove the tape once the final coat is touch dry, so it doesn’t pull at the edges.

With an aerosol, shake the can well, hold it the distance the can recommends and build up several light passes, starting and stopping the spray off the radiator. Mask a wide area and ventilate the room well.

You don’t need to paint the back of a radiator unless you can see it. The wall behind it is a different matter: paint it before the radiator if you are decorating the room, using the long-handled roller or radiator brush.

How long before you can turn the heating back on?

Most radiator paints are touch dry within a few hours, but the paint needs longer to harden before it gets hot. As a rule, wait at least 24 hours after the final coat, and longer if the tin says so or the room is cold and damp. When you do switch on, start on a low setting. A faint smell the first time the radiator heats up is normal and should fade with the windows open.

In a Welsh winter, a day without heating is not always welcome. Painting one radiator at a time, or doing the job in late spring or early autumn, makes it far easier. For drying times on walls and woodwork, see how long it takes to paint a room.

Does painting a radiator reduce its heat output?

Not in any way you would notice. Most of a radiator’s heat reaches the room by warming the air around it, so normal paint in any colour, dark or light, makes very little difference. The things to avoid are metallic paints, which can reduce output, and many thick layers built up over the years. On an old radiator with heavy build-up, stripping back is better than adding another coat.

Should you paint a radiator on the wall or take it off?

Most radiators can be painted in place with a radiator brush and mini roller, and that is how most decorators do it. Taking a radiator off gives you easier access to every side and to the wall behind, but it means isolating and draining it, then refitting, refilling and bleeding it afterwards and checking for leaks. Unless you are confident with plumbing, leave that part to a qualified plumber or heating engineer.

Chrome, stainless steel and polished designer radiators are best left unpainted, as paint struggles to bond to them and a factory finish may lose its warranty once painted. Frequently asked questions

Can you paint a radiator with normal emulsion?+

No. Wall emulsion isn’t made to cope with heat and will crack, discolour and wear through on a radiator. Use radiator enamel, a radiator aerosol or a metal paint the manufacturer says is suitable for radiators.

Do you need to prime a radiator before painting?+

Only where there is bare metal or treated rust. Sound, previously painted radiators just need cleaning and a light sand to give the new paint a key. Spot-prime bare patches with a metal primer, or prime the whole radiator if it is bare.

How long after painting a radiator can I turn the heating on?+

Wait at least 24 hours after the final coat, or longer if the tin says so or the room is cold. Then start the heating on a low setting.

Can you paint a radiator without taking it off the wall?+

Yes. Most radiators are painted in place using a long-handled radiator brush or roller for the gaps and a mini roller for the face. Mask the valves and slide card behind it to protect the wall.

Can you paint a chrome radiator?+

It’s best not to. Paint struggles to grip chrome and polished metal, so it tends to peel, and painting a designer radiator can affect its warranty. If you want a new colour, a replacement radiator is usually the better option.
