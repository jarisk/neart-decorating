Kitchen respraying vs painting your cabinets yourself

Updated 21 November 2026By Neart Decorating, Cardiff8 min read
Quick answer

If your units are solid and you only want a new colour, both routes work, but they suit different people. Painting the cabinets yourself costs roughly £100–£250 in materials and a week or more of evenings and drying time. A professional respray of an average kitchen typically costs around £1,000–£2,500 and gives a smooth, factory-like finish that lasts far longer. Both are a fraction of the cost of a new kitchen.

How much does a kitchen respray cost?

Most kitchen sprayers price by the number of pieces being sprayed: every door, drawer front, end panel, plinth and cornice. Across the UK in 2026, that typically works out at around £50–£90 per door or drawer front, and £1,000–£2,500 for an average kitchen of about ten units. Larger kitchens, islands and tall larder units add to the total.

What moves the price:

  • The number of pieces. This matters most. A galley kitchen with twelve doors costs much less than a large kitchen with an island.
  • The current finish. Bare wood and previously painted doors are the most straightforward. Gloss and laminate need extra preparation and a bonding primer to grip.
  • Condition. Chips, swollen edges by the sink or dishwasher, and old grease build-up all need putting right before any colour goes on.
  • Extras. Spraying the island, the end panels, plinths and cornices, or changing the handles at the same time.

What painting kitchen cabinets yourself really involves

Painting your own cabinets is a popular DIY job, and it can turn out well. But the painting is the quick part. Most of the work is preparation, and that is what decides whether the paint lasts. A typical DIY job looks like this:

  1. Take everything apart. Remove doors, drawer fronts and handles, and label each one so it goes back in the same place.
  2. Degrease thoroughly. Kitchen doors carry years of cooking grease, especially near the hob. Paint will not stick to it.
  3. Sand to give a key. A light sand all over so the primer can grip, then remove every bit of dust.
  4. Prime. Use a primer made for the surface: a bonding primer for gloss, laminate or melamine.
  5. Paint both sides. Two thin coats on each side, with drying time between coats and before turning each door over.
  6. Let it cure. Paint can be dry to the touch in hours but take days or weeks to fully harden, so the first fortnight is when most knocks and chips happen.

Materials for an average kitchen usually come to £100–£250: degreaser, abrasives, primer, a good cupboard or furniture paint, fine rollers and brushes. Because each door has two sides and several coats, most people find the job stretches over a week or more of evenings, with the kitchen half-dismantled throughout.

Where DIY usually goes wrong. Skipping the degreasing or sanding, using ordinary wall paint, putting coats on too thickly, and rehanging doors before the paint has hardened. These lead to drips, brush marks, sticky doors and paint that chips around the handles within months.

How long does each finish last?

Durability comes down to preparation and the paint system rather than the colour. A professionally sprayed kitchen, properly cleaned, sanded and primed, and finished in a hard-wearing coating, stands up to everyday cooking and cleaning for many years.

A DIY brush-and-roller finish can last well if the preparation is thorough and a proper cupboard paint is used. In practice, the areas that see the most use, such as the doors under the sink, around the bin and by the handles, are usually the first to show wear. Touching those up is easy, but expect to do it.

Which kitchens can be resprayed?

  • Solid wood and oak kitchens: respray very well. The grain can show through, or it can be filled for a smoother modern look.
  • MDF and previously painted kitchens: ideal for spraying.
  • Gloss and laminate doors: can often be sprayed with the right preparation and a bonding primer.
  • Vinyl-wrapped doors: fine if the wrap is sound. If it is already peeling at the edges, the wrap usually needs removing first, and new doors may be better value.

Kitchen cabinet colours that won’t date

Trends come and go quickly in kitchens. The cold greys and high-gloss whites that were everywhere a few years ago are exactly what people now want to change. If you plan to keep the kitchen for a long time, these choices age most gracefully:

  • Warm off-whites and creams: softer than brilliant white, easy to live with and good in smaller or darker kitchens.
  • Deep greens: from sage to dark racing green. They suit shaker doors and pair well with wood and brass.
  • Navy and inky blues: classic and smart, especially on lower units or an island.
  • Warm neutrals: mushroom, stone, taupe and greige are the natural replacement for cool grey.
  • Soft black or charcoal: striking on an island or in a large, bright kitchen.

Two practical tips: choose a satin or eggshell sheen rather than high gloss, as it hides fingerprints and small marks far better; and look at samples in your own kitchen in daylight and in the evening, against your actual worktop and floor.

Two-tone kitchens, walls and worktops

A two-tone kitchen usually puts the darker colour on the base units or island and the lighter colour on the wall units. That keeps the room feeling open while grounding it with depth. Common combinations are navy with off-white, deep green with cream, and charcoal with warm white.

There is no rule that cabinets must be lighter or darker than the walls, but contrast is what makes a kitchen look intentional. Dark units look best against lighter walls, and pale units against a stronger wall colour or a dark worktop. The same goes for worktops: a pale quartz or marble-effect top lifts dark cabinets, and a dark worktop gives definition to light ones.

Frequently asked questions

Can you paint kitchen cabinets without sanding?+

Not if you want the finish to last. Kitchen doors are coated in grease and often have a smooth factory finish, so paint needs a clean, keyed surface and the right primer to grip. Skipping sanding is the most common reason DIY cabinet paint chips and peels.

Can laminate or high-gloss kitchen doors be resprayed?+

Often, yes. Laminate and gloss doors can be sprayed with the right preparation and a specialist bonding primer. Vinyl-wrapped doors that are already peeling usually need the wrap removing first. We check your units and tell you honestly what will work before quoting.

Can I use the kitchen while it is being resprayed?+

For part of the job, usually yes. The rooms are masked and protected, and we plan the work so you are without a usable kitchen for as little time as possible. Your written quote sets out the expected timescale.

Do you respray the cabinet carcasses inside?+

Usually only the visible parts are sprayed: doors, drawer fronts, end panels, plinths, cornices and any island. The insides of the cupboards are normally left as they are, which keeps the cost down.

Is a respray worth it in a rental property?+

For many landlords, yes. A respray is a quick way to refresh a tired rental kitchen between tenancies for far less than a refit, and the units, worktops and appliances stay where they are.
